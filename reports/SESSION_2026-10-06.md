## Priority 1 — H17: thresholds derived on subscription

**Do not implement H16. It had the direction backwards.** It recommended raising L2's
cut-off and halving the bids; the data says fire **more than twice as often**.

### 1a / 1b — in-sample against out-of-sample

No model, no standardiser, no calibrator — a monotone threshold on observed Total
subscription, scored against the realised listing gain, 620 mainboard IPOs, 2010–2026.

| Target | Method | Threshold | Fires | Hits | Delivered | Wilson 95% LB |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L1** (promise 90%) | in-sample | 16.86x | 284 | 256 | **90.1%** | 86.1% |
| | walk-forward | per year | 300 | 259 | **86.3%** | 82.0% |
| **L2** (promise 80%) | in-sample | 26.66x | 248 | 200 | **80.6%** | 75.3% |
| | walk-forward | per year | 244 | 185 | **75.8%** | 70.1% |

**The flattery is +3.8pp on L1 and +4.8pp on L2.** In-sample, the rule picks the point that
looks best on the rows it is looking at. **Trust the walk-forward number** — it is the only
one that answers "what will this deliver on an IPO I have not seen", which is the only
question a bid decision asks.

**1b is refuted, not confirmed.** You expected L1 to hold; at its own naively derived
threshold it delivers **86.3%**, the same disease as L2 and only 1pp milder. It looked safe
in H16 because H16 measured the *model's probability cut*, which sits at a different and
much higher subscription level — a different quantity, not a healthier one.

### Where the mechanism shows

L2's naively derived threshold **drifts downward** — 31.11x (2015) to 17.20x (2025) — pulled
by the hot 2021–24 market. Then it breaks:

| Year | Derived on prior years | IPOs | Fires | Hits | Delivered |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 19.29x | 63 | 37 | 30 | 81% |
| 2024 | 17.41x | 80 | 51 | 41 | 80% |
| **2025** | **17.20x** | **102** | **60** | **36** | **60%** |
| 2026 | 26.51x | 20 | 3 | 1 | 33% |

2025 is the largest year in the dataset and carries most of the shortfall alone. Same
pathology H12 found in the 2-year half-life: recent hot years dominate and mislead. A
threshold that re-derives itself downward every year is not learning, it is chasing.

### 1c — the answer, in the units the email prints

Calibrated by deriving at a stricter internal target until pooled walk-forward delivery
meets the promise (L1 95% → 90.2%; L2 87% → 80.8%):

> ### L1 fires above Total ≥ 28.39x
> ### L2 fires above Total ≥ 54.02x

Subscription levels you can read off a broker screen. No probability is involved in the
decision any more.

### H16 reversed — the shipped model was grossly over-conservative

| Target | Rule | Total ≥ | Fires | Delivered | Share of IPOs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | production model (probability cut) | 64.86x | 135 | 99.3% | 21.8% |
| L1 | **H17** | **28.39x** | **242** | 95.0% | **39.0%** |
| L2 | production model (probability cut) | 98.53x | 74 | 98.6% | 11.9% |
| L2 | **H17** | **54.02x** | **166** | 88.0% | **26.8%** |

The shipped L2 fired on **11.9%** of IPOs and delivered **98.6%** against an **80%**
promise. It was not under-delivering — it was leaving most of the opportunity on the table,
and H16 would have made that worse.

**Both failures share one root cause: routing the decision through a calibrated
probability.** H16's shortfall came from `retrain_l.evaluate()` refitting the *calibrator*
on each window; the refit calibrator places `p_cal ≥ 0.80` far lower on the subscription
scale, which is why that window fired on 49% of its test rows rather than 11.9%. So H16
measured the refit procedure while 98.53x describes the shipped bundle — two different
thresholds, one under-delivering and one over-conservative. Removing the intermediary
removes both.

### 1d — what the honesty costs

Nothing. It buys opportunities.

* **L1**: 135 → 242 fires, ~7.9 → **~14.2 a year**
* **L2**: 74 → 166 fires, ~4.4 → **~9.8 a year**

L2 at 54.02x by year: 2021 91%, 2023 95%, 2024 92%, **2025 76%**, 2026 100%. 2025 still
misses 80%, but at 76% rather than the 60% the naive threshold gave.

### 1e / 1f

**H17 recorded, superseding H16, awaiting ratification.** Nothing in the live path uses
these levels yet. Until ratified, every L2 **BID** carries, verbatim:

```

```

Asserted byte-exact in the self-test; present only under a BID, absent under a "no"; L1

### 2d — the standing rule

> **A newly listed IPO's price is captured ON ITS LISTING DATE. Never earlier, never later.**

Wired into the 17:30 job. Not a preference: a price read later has been silently restated
for any split or bonus since — Syngene's real ₹295 reads ₹147.50 once its 2017 bonus
applies, turning +18% into −41%. A bhavcopy *for the listing date* is frozen at what traded
that day.

`capture_listing_day()` refuses a future date outright, and refuses a past one unless
`--catch-up` is passed. Catch-up is safe for the same reason: it reads that date's
**archived** bhavcopy, never a current quote.

## Three bugs in my own work, found by checking

**The bhavcopy cache was empty on every run.** `to_parquet` raised `ImportError` (pyarrow is
not in `requirements.txt`) inside a bare `except: pass`, so each re-run refetched all 519
dates from the exchanges — slow, and a worse citizen than intended. Now `csv.gz`, and a
cache failure is **logged** rather than swallowed. A cache that silently does not cache is
worse than no cache, because nothing tells you.

**A wrong-scrip match produced a −92% label.** Dharan Infra EPC (offer ₹180) matched a BSE
row opening at ₹14.20, because BSE's old-format bhavcopy pads and **truncates** scrip names
so a 7-character prefix can land on an unrelated company. `find()` now returns **which key
matched**, and a lone prefix-only match gets its own status
`single_source_prefix_match_unverified`, so it can be excluded without excluding every
single-source row. This is match *provenance*, not a judgement about whether a number looks
plausible — **plausibility inference stays forbidden (H8)**.

**The first backfill silently dropped 313 companies.** `targets()` preferred the master's
`listing_date`, which holds only 310 of 623 rows and starts in Oct 2021. Per the known
defect it is `issue_open_date` that actually carries the listing date, for all 623 rows back
to 2010 — and the two are identical wherever both exist. The self-test now asserts the
target set spans 2010 to 2026.

**And one guard I will not claim works:** `first_day_marker` (`PREVCLOSE == 0`) is False on
455 of 457 correctly-labelled rows, because only BSE's old format zeroes it. It is recorded
but must not be relied on.
